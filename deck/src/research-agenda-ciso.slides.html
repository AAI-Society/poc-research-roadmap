Proof-of-Control
For the people who sign off

The research agenda, told for security leaders: what an AI agent can prove about itself today, what it cannot yet, and what we are building to close the gap.

Moving trust at the speed of machines

Research roadmap · September 2026

Trust that can be checked is the only kind that scales.

Every other kind asks you to take an institution’s word for it, which is exactly what stops working when the actors are machines acting thousands of times a minute.

Proof-of-Control, in one breath

An open standard for checking what an AI agent actually did, not what it says it did

Every action an agent takes produces a signed receipt. Anyone with the published tools can check the receipt, so the vendor’s word is no longer the only evidence you have. The working draft is public, and it is being built in the open.

01

The problem

Why “trust us” stops working with agents.

Three shifts

Who initiates the interaction has changed twice. It is changing again.

01 · Until the 1990s

Human → Human

Trust ran on relationships, institutions, and reputation. A person stood behind every action.

02 · Last three decades

Human → Machine

Digital transformation and SaaS. Software waits to be told; a human initiates and can be asked what happened.

03 · Now

Machine → Machine

Agents initiate, at machine scale, without a human in the loop. Trust has to come from evidence.

Each shift moved trust further from the person who carries the accountability. This one moves it out of reach of after-the-fact review entirely.

Four ways to know what an AI did

Most of today’s AI assurance sits on the bottom two rungs

The ladder is the shared scale. Every vendor claim, every audit, and every control you buy lands somewhere on it.

1Assertiontheir word: a policy document, a dashboard, a promise in the contract
2Attestationa third party vouches: an auditor, a certification, a SOC 2 report

Proof-of-Control begins here — the trusted party is removed

3Independently verifiableanyone can check the evidence with published tools
4Self-enforcingthe action cannot happen unless the check passes

The standard’s own wording for level 3 says “there is nobody left to trust.” Building it showed that is not quite true, and that is the first thing the research programme fixes

Even hardware proof rests on somebody · P01

“Nobody left to trust” turned out to be five parties, and four of them can fail silently

A confidential-computing chip can prove which code ran. To believe that proof, here is who you are trusting, in plain terms, and whether you would ever find out if they let you down.

WhoWhat you are taking on faithWould you find out?
The chip makerThe processor and its security firmware behave as documentedNever
Their certificate serviceThe revocation and version data you download to check a proof is genuineWithin ~12 hours
The chip’s signing componentIt measures honestly and never leaks its signing keyNever
Your cloud providerThe host does not reach into protected memory, and the firmware it reports is the firmware it runsNever
Whoever publishes the “known good” valuesThe reference you compare against really belongs to the code you believe it doesNever

Why this matters to you: a control you cannot name the trust behind is a control you cannot defend to a regulator · parallax, a shipped tool, now computes this list from a description of any deployment

Measured on Intel TDX, a confidential VM on Google Cloud · P03

Asking the hardware for proof takes longer than the action it is proving

So no deployment can ask per action. Proofs are taken once and reused, which means every action rests on a proof taken earlier. If the code changed in between, nothing in the receipt tells you.

We built the auditors before the receipt.

Seven tools have shipped. Five of them check claims. None of them yet produces the complete, signed receipt those checks are meant to read. Closing that gap is the centre of the roadmap.

The receipt, field by field · what the shipped tools cover

Twenty-one fields make an action checkable. Today, tools compute five of them and write down none.

The values are calculated and discarded. Nothing assembles them, signs them, or links each receipt to the one before it. That is not a research problem; it is the next thing to build.

Run against the standard’s own best example receipt, the audit tool can establish two fields out of twenty-two, and both of those are identity fields, which is a privacy cost as much as a security property

Traction

What has shipped, and what it taught us.

Since the working draft · August to September 2026

3
questions answered, each with a working open-source tool anyone can run
7
tools shipped, four of them the roadmap did not anticipate
8
defects found in the standard by building against it, before anyone deployed on it

Every tool is public under github.com/AAI-Society · every number in this deck traces to a measurement or a document in the open repository

The one thing an operator can deploy today

A connection that refuses unless the hardware proves what code is on the other end. Verified, end to end.

Two shipped tools, no changes to the application on either side. This is narrow, and it is real.

What it gives you

The one place in the programme where an operator gets a real, end-to-end, checked property rather than a claim.

What it does not, stated plainly

Google Cloud only. Intel TDX only. It produces a certificate, not a receipt. It has been tested on five deployments we wrote ourselves and no real-world system yet.

Eight defects in the standard, found by trying to build against it

Reading a specification finds typos. Building against it finds the things that would have failed in production.

Six of the eight are invisible to the standard’s own validator: the receipt passes every check and still fails to carry the property the field exists for. Three examples, in plain terms.

01The reference example was wrongThe standard’s own strongest sample receipt carries a hardware measurement of the wrong size for the chip it names. Anyone who copied it would have inherited the error.
02The tamper-evident chain protected three fields, not the receiptEverything else could be altered without breaking a link. The fix costs nothing: chain the whole receipt. It is drafted and waiting to be filed.
03A required anti-replay field nobody could honestly fillThe standard requires a challenge from the relying party, and nothing in the ecosystem issues one. A challenge the agent mints for itself defeats no replay, and the validator cannot tell.

All eight are documented in the open, with the change each one asks of the standard · the same method put three requirements into the standard in the first place

05

Institutional

P11 · P12

Who pays for verification, and why assurance regimes fail

Ranked last because they are not technical, and possibly the most determinative work here. Certificate Transparency won because browsers mandated it, not because the cryptography was elegant.

Decisions, not engineering

Two things nobody can code around

Both are real trade-offs a working group has to decide, and both get sharper the moment the recorder exists. Security leaders are the right people in the room for them.

A complete audit trail links every action to every other

The chain that makes the receipt tamper-evident also makes every action an agent takes traceable to every other one, by construction. That is the opposite of the privacy property one of our own papers argues for. Building the recorder turns this from a footnote into a choice.

Fail open, or fail closed

Two of the shipped tools refuse requests that fail their checks. A third never refuses, on the principle that a meter must not be able to take down the service it measures. Put all three in one request path and there is no agreed answer to what happens when a check cannot complete: availability or enforcement.

What we need from the people in this room

01A real system to run it onEvery result so far comes from five deployments we wrote ourselves. One production workload behind the confidential-VM path, with the audit tool run on its records, would be worth more than the next paper.
02Tell us what your auditors would acceptThe two papers on verification economics and assurance failure are ranked last and may matter most. They need practitioners who have lived through SOC 2, ISO and FedRAMP, not more literature.
03Hardware beyond one cloudEvery measurement is one chip on one cloud. The apparatus deploys, measures and tears itself down; it needs somewhere else to run.
04Sit on the decisionsAudit trail against privacy, fail-open against fail-closed, how fine-grained the log should be. These are working-group calls, and they need people who carry the accountability.
05Argue with the orderThe priorities are one person’s assessment, written down so they can be contested. If your risk register ranks these differently, that is the input we are asking for.
